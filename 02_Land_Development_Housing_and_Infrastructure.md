# Land, Development, Housing, and Infrastructure

## 1. Land Expansion

The city can expand by:

- Purchasing land
- Annexing land
- Preparing land for development
- Listing city-controlled land for private purchase
- Retaining land for public construction

Private companies can buy land that the city has made available and develop it within approved zoning and planning limits.

## 2. Development Paths

There are two primary development paths.

### A. Private Development

The city lists available land and shapes the opportunity through:

- Zoning
- Infrastructure
- Asking price
- Incentives
- Approval requirements

A private company then submits a compliant proposal.

Possible private developers include:

- Housing developers
- Apartment developers
- Industrial employers
- Commercial employers
- Media companies
- Other specialized institutions

### B. City-Built Development

The city can directly build on development-ready land.

For city housing, the player:

- Zones or designates the land
- Chooses the housing type
- Starts construction directly

City-built housing behaves more like a conventional city-builder placement system, but construction is slower and funded with city capital.

City ownership provides:

- Greater control
- Lower maintenance risk
- Lower fire or neglect risk
- Direct responsibility for operating and capital costs

## 3. Zoning Choices

For privately developed land, the city may designate:

- Single-family housing
- Mixed-use development
- High-density development
- Apartments
- Multiple acceptable uses, allowing the developer to decide

The city controls legal land use but does not design every private building.

## 4. Proposal Structure

Private development should generally produce **two or three complete proposals**.

Proposal differences may include:

- Density
- Target income or property-value band
- Construction speed
- Estimated tax revenue
- Ownership structure
- Infrastructure demand
- Risk
- Long-term maintenance assumptions

The city can choose one proposal or reject all proposals.

There should not initially be a detailed negotiation or counteroffer loop.

## 5. Binding Approval

Once the city approves a specific proposal, that proposal becomes contractually binding.

The developer should not freely switch to a substantially different plan after approval.

## 6. Rejection and Developer Interest

Rejecting all proposals leaves the property undeveloped and allows it to be relisted later.

Repeated rejection reduces **property-specific developer interest**.

Interest can recover through:

- City growth
- Improved infrastructure
- Higher demand
- Increased land value
- Better reputation
- Broader zoning
- Reduced asking price
- Incentives

The penalty is tied primarily to the property and development opportunity rather than permanently blacklisting the entire city.

## 7. Incentive Packages

The city can use a simple preset system:

- **Low**
- **Balanced**
- **Aggressive**

The same interface can apply across development types.

The exact financial impact may remain partially hidden. The interface can instead summarize important factors as:

- Strong
- Neutral
- Weak

The player may change the incentive package when relisting.

Difficulty interaction:

- **Easy/Normal:** Changing incentives does not reset the proposal timer.
- **Hard:** Changing incentives resets the proposal timer but slightly improves the chance of receiving a better offer.

The interface should communicate improved odds without displaying an exact probability.

## 8. Development-Ready Infrastructure

The city can build roads and utilities before private development begins.

This can:

- Increase developer interest
- Improve proposal quality
- Reduce construction delay
- Shape parcel layout
- Reduce infrastructure uncertainty

By default, city-built infrastructure remains city-owned and city-maintained.

The city may later sell infrastructure or related property rights.

## 9. Infrastructure Ownership

Infrastructure ownership affects:

- Maintenance responsibility
- Capital replacement
- Emergency response
- Development leverage
- Liability
- Redevelopment control

Selling infrastructure may reduce city costs but also reduce control.

## 10. Private Redevelopment

A private owner may seek to redevelop property without changing its broad zoning category.

Example:

- Existing single-family low-income housing
- Proposed single-family high-income redevelopment

A major demolition and rebuild still requires city approval.

The developer submits two or three complete options. The city can approve one or reject all.

## 11. Consequences of Redevelopment Rejection

If all redevelopment options are rejected:

- The existing neighborhood remains
- A fixed resubmission delay begins
- Developer dissatisfaction rises
- Negative public relations may occur
- The owner may reduce investment
- Maintenance may decline
- Neighborhood condition may worsen
- The developer may offer to sell the property to the city

## 12. City Purchase of Private Property

A developer or owner may request that the city purchase the property.

The city can accept only if the budget allows.

Forced condemnation is not part of the initial general redevelopment system.

## 13. Emergency Condemnation

The Emergency Commander may condemn property when an immediate life-safety threat requires action.

The Commander can order demolition or emergency rebuilding without prior player approval.

This power is limited to urgent safety conditions.

Payment responsibility is assigned initially according to:

- Ownership
- Responsibility
- Known cause
- Emergency rules

Disputes are resolved afterward through the Court system.

## 14. Development Quality and Occupancy

Development quality should interact with local demand.

A high-quality project placed far above the area's economic level may experience:

- Slow occupancy
- Increased housing costs
- Higher property values
- Tax pressure
- Resident dissatisfaction
- Displacement
- Attraction of higher-income citizens

A lower-quality development may fill faster but create future maintenance and service risks.

## 15. Income Mix

Private housing projects can generate a mix of income/value bands rather than being perfectly homogeneous.

The mix is influenced by:

- Demand
- Development quality
- Local land value
- Accessibility
- Nearby employment
- Services
- Developer strategy
- Regional conditions

## 16. Media Development as a Special Proposal Type

Private developers can request approval for:

- Newspaper facilities
- Radio stations
- Television outlets

The city may also build its own media facilities.

The city reviews land use and development approval but does not select the outlet's political or editorial position.

Repeated rejection of media proposals may create public suspicion, especially when comparable non-media projects are approved.

A single rejection for legitimate planning reasons should not automatically create a major penalty.
