# Game Vision and Core Structure

## 1. Core Premise

The game is a sandbox city-management simulation in which the player serves as the **City Manager**.

The player is not an unrestricted city-building authority. Instead, the player manages departments, budgets, land policy, public construction, private proposals, emergency coordination, legal exposure, and relationships with political leadership.

The city should feel like a living institution with competing interests rather than a construction canvas.

## 2. Player Role

The City Manager is responsible for:

- Department funding and operating policy
- Routine capital purchases
- Service priorities
- Public development and city-owned facilities
- Reviewing private development proposals
- Emergency coordination
- Responding to warnings, recommendations, and crises
- Managing reputation and public accountability
- Working under the Mayor and, for major decisions, the City Council

The player does not personally direct every worker, vehicle, judge, developer, citizen, or media outlet.

## 3. Mayor and Political Leadership

The Mayor acts as an AI-driven source of:

- Political pressure
- Strategic guidance
- Approval or rejection of major expenditures
- Public expectations
- Personality-driven priorities

The starting **region** and the starting **Mayor** are selected separately. This allows the same physical region to produce different political challenges.

Major or debt-funded projects may require City Council approval. Routine purchases remain within the City Manager's authority.

## 4. Sandbox Structure

The game has no standard campaign victory condition.

The primary experience is continued city growth, management, and institutional development. The player may define personal goals through city size, economic strength, public service quality, reputation, resilience, or development style.

The city can still fail catastrophically.

## 5. Catastrophic Game Over

A catastrophic destruction threshold can end the city immediately.

Proposed thresholds:

- **Easy:** 100% destruction
- **Normal:** 70% destruction
- **Hard:** 50% destruction

The percentage should be based primarily on:

- Occupied buildings
- Critical infrastructure
- Functional developed areas

It should not be based on undeveloped map area.

There is no recovery window once the threshold is reached.

## 6. Final Failure Report

A failed city receives a detailed final report containing:

- Percentage of the developed city destroyed
- Population loss and displacement
- Buildings and infrastructure lost
- Incident timeline
- Alerts ignored
- Alerts acknowledged but not resolved
- Actions taken
- Manager and department recommendations
- Departments that became overwhelmed
- Potentially preventable causes
- Reputation and economic effects

The objective is to make failure understandable and narratively meaningful.

## 7. Historical City Archive

Failed cities are archived as historical scenarios or references.

Later playthroughs may mention them through:

- Newspaper stories
- Radio or television retrospectives
- Policy comparisons
- Historical disaster references
- Public warnings

Successful cities should also be archived.

The archive is initially narrative rather than a system that directly changes future mechanics.

## 8. Regional Presets

Regional presets influence:

- Visual identity
- Climate
- Infrastructure assumptions
- Planning rules
- Economic tendencies
- Environmental pressures
- Development patterns

The regional system should remain data-driven and manageable rather than becoming a full real-world legal simulator.

## 9. Organic City Form

A major design distinction from conventional city builders is the avoidance of rigid painted zoning grids.

The city instead uses:

- Domains or broader planning areas
- Procedurally generated parcels
- Roads
- Easements
- Yards
- Fences
- Driveways
- Development plans

Private development should generate realistic irregular layouts within city rules.

Public facilities such as fire stations remain directly placeable and can snap to roads.

## 10. Development Quality

Buildings and developments can have approximately **three to six quality levels**.

Quality affects:

- Construction value
- Condition
- Attractiveness
- Occupancy
- Maintenance
- Tax value
- Fire and safety risk
- Neighborhood expectations

Constructing development far above the surrounding city's average may produce:

- Slow occupancy
- Resident anger
- Rising taxes
- Rising property values
- Displacement pressure

## 11. Income and Property Bands

Proposed income/value bands:

1. Poverty
2. Near poverty
3. Low income
4. Lower middle
5. Middle
6. Upper middle
7. Upper class

Developments can contain a generated mix of these bands based on demand and proposal type.

Citizens can move between income bands over time.

## 12. Neighborhood Change

Neighborhood condition changes according to:

- Service access
- Infrastructure quality
- Vacancy
- Crime
- Investment
- Maintenance
- Employment
- Education
- Development pressure
- Property ownership behavior

Rezoning generally requires demolition and rebuilding rather than instantly transforming existing structures. This can create temporary chaos, displacement, service disruption, and political consequences.

## 13. Primary Gameplay Identity

The intended gameplay identity is:

> Manage a city as an accountable institution in which independent residents, developers, departments, judges, media companies, and political leaders respond to the player's decisions.

The challenge is not simply placing the correct building. It is balancing:

- Authority
- Capacity
- Cost
- Time
- Uncertainty
- Public trust
- Legal exposure
- Political approval
- Unequal consequences
